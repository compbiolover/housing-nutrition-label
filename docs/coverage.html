Where the label reads the assessor's record

Everywhere, the label models a building from national data: a structure inventory and the census tract's year-built distribution. Where a county or state publishes its assessment roll, the label reads what the assessor observed instead — the actual year built, floor area and, in some places, stories, walls, foundation and condition. This page shows where that is true today.

Is the right house found?

The dangerous failure is not a missing record but a neighbor's record shown as yours. Every source is checked end to end: homes drawn at random from the source itself, geocoded exactly as the label does, then looked up. When the address and the parcel do not agree, the label declines to guess and keeps its modeled value.

What this page does and does not say

  • A source is not a guarantee. Inside a covered county the label still falls back to its modeled values when the address cannot be matched to exactly one parcel, when the record omits a field, or when the assessor's service is slow. The "answered" rates above are the realistic share.
  • Serving is not measuring. Only the jurisdictions on the accuracy page carry measured accuracy figures. The others carry a verification run that checks the right parcel is found, which is a narrower claim.
  • Homes are ACS housing units in each source's counties, the same table the label uses for its year-built fallback. Some sources do not answer for every home in those counties (New York State's public layer, for instance, covers only the counties that opted in, and it is mapped that way).
  • Nothing is bundled. Every record is queried live from the publisher and cached briefly; owner names, mailing addresses and sale prices are never requested.
  • What you enter on the label always wins over the assessor, and the assessor wins over the model.
